I've watched organisations with brilliant strategies fail to execute them. And organisations with ordinary strategies execute brilliantly. The difference is almost never the strategy itself. It's the operating model—how work actually flows, who owns what, and whether the system reinforces or undermines strategic intent.

Most leaders treat operating model design as a consequence of strategy, something that resolves itself once you've decided what to do. But that's backwards. Your operating model either enables your strategy or constrains it. Get it wrong, and you'll spend years wondering why your vision never became reality.

Strategy is the what. Operating model is the how. And the how determines whether your what ever actually happens.

## What Actually Makes an Operating Model Work

An operating model is really about seven interconnected design choices based on the [McKinsey 7S framework](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/enduring-ideas/the-7-s-framework). But here's the thing most organisations miss: you don't need perfection in all seven. You need alignment.

**Start with shared values.** Culture isn't about perks or casual Fridays. It's the values and norms that shape behaviour when no one is watching. I worked with a financial services firm that claimed to value "innovation" but rewarded risk-averse behaviour. Within eighteen months, innovation initiatives stalled completely—not because people didn't want to innovate, but because incentives punished it. When stated values contradict how you actually reward people, they stop believing in the mission.

**Then define roles and accountability.** Ambiguity about who owns what is the enemy of execution. Every outcome needs one person who wakes up at night worrying about it—not a committee. Use frameworks like [RACI or DACI matrices](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/raci-matrix) to clarify decision rights. I watched a bank restructure around customer segments instead of products. On paper, it made sense—you get closer to customers. What actually happened: three product teams each implemented their own data pipelines (no shared infrastructure), customer service received requests from six different "owners" with conflicting priorities, and nobody could explain why the same customer received different pricing in different segments. Eighteen months later they reorganised back. The lesson: unclear ownership doesn't just create confusion. It creates expensive, duplicative work.

**Processes matter less than most think, but the critical ones matter enormously.** Don't design them function-by-function. Trace how value actually flows from customer need to outcome. Most rework happens at handoffs—where work passes between teams. I've seen firms where a customer request required sign-off from five different functions, each with their own quality gate, each adding days to resolution time. The customer didn't care about any of those handoffs. They just wanted an answer. When we collapsed unnecessary handoffs and made the critical ones crystal clear, resolution time dropped from 15 days to 3. Not because people worked harder. Because the process stopped fighting itself.

**Metrics direct human attention with remarkable precision.** If you measure X but reward Y, you'll get Y. Always. One investment firm measured quality of loan underwriting but paid traders on volume of loans originated. Guess what they optimized for? Quality tanked. The company learned the hard way: the metric you choose will drive behavior you can't override with exhortation. Use [balanced scorecards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_scorecard)—financial, customer, operational, and people metrics. If you only track money, you'll chase short-term revenue at the expense of culture, retention, and capability. You'll win the quarter and lose the war.

**Data ownership is non-negotiable.** Assign someone accountable for each critical dataset's quality, timeliness, and access. Follow [data governance frameworks](https://www.dama-dmbok.org/) to establish clear ownership. I've seen analytics teams spend weeks debugging reports, only to discover the data had been wrong for months because nobody owned quality. Once we assigned clear ownership and made that person's quarterly goals include "data quality metrics," accuracy improved dramatically. Not because people suddenly got more competent. Because someone was now accountable.

**Finally, invest in people—especially frontline managers.** They're the translation layer between strategy and execution. If they understand the operating model and can explain it, coach people through it, and make good decisions within it, you'll succeed. If they don't, you won't. Most organisations underinvest here.

## Why Operating Model Alignment Actually Matters

The insight most organisations miss: all pieces must reinforce each other. You can have clear roles but broken processes—people know what they own but can't do the work. You can have brilliant processes but misaligned metrics—teams follow procedures perfectly but optimise for the wrong outcomes. You can have great values but silos—people believe in collaboration but the org chart prevents it.

One technology firm I worked with had exceptional people, clear roles, and smart processes. But their metrics system was built on individual performance, not team outcomes. Despite claiming to value cross-functional collaboration, the metrics incentivised hoarding knowledge and avoiding shared work. It took two years and significant restructuring to fix. This is why piecemeal changes often fail. You can't fix structure without addressing process. You can't implement collaborative processes with individualistic metrics. Every element either reinforces or undermines the others.

## Operating Models Under Pressure: The AI Factor

AI is forcing a reckoning with operating models in ways most organisations haven't anticipated. It fundamentally challenges the assumptions your operating model was built on.

Traditional operating models assume expertise is scarce and hierarchically controlled. But AI commoditises certain types of analysis and decision-making. I've seen credit underwriting teams where a junior analyst with an AI scoring tool could assess risk better than senior teams had historically. The org chart became a constraint rather than an enabler. Organisations that don't redesign around this will waste senior talent on work that junior people with better tools can do.

They also assume processes are stable enough to design once and refine slowly. But AI accelerates change velocity. Organisations need operating models that evolve faster—with feedback loops in weeks, not quarters, and autonomy for teams to adjust processes as conditions change.

Governance gaps emerge quickly. When an AI system recommends pricing or credit decisions, who's accountable if it's wrong? Traditional role clarity breaks down when algorithmic decisions muddy accountability. You need new roles and [governance frameworks for AI](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework)—model owners, ethics reviewers—that most organisations haven't yet designed.

Finally, data becomes central at scale. Your operating model's data component must ensure quality and appropriate access—not just visibility. I've seen AI projects fail not because algorithms were bad, but because data ownership was unclear and governance was either too loose or too tight. Without clear data accountability, AI systems amplify existing biases and errors at scale.

Finally, skill requirements shift dramatically. Organisations that need data engineers, ML practitioners, and people who can bridge business and technical worlds don't work with training programs built for yesterday's skills. You need continuous learning loops embedded in work, not annual refreshers. And you need the humility to know that what you're teaching today might be obsolete in six months.

## Where Most Organisations Actually Go Wrong

The biggest mistake I've seen is reorganizing without transforming. Redrawing the org chart feels productive—it's visible, fast, creates urgency. But if you don't change processes, incentives, and decision-making simultaneously, people revert to old patterns within weeks. You've rearranged boxes, not changed how people actually work.

I watched a firm completely restructure around market segments. New org chart, new roles, new reporting lines. But they didn't change the annual budget cycle, which was still organized by function. They didn't change how technology decisions got made—still centralized. They didn't change metrics—still individual performance based. Within three months, the new structure had adapted to the old processes. The reorganisation cost millions and delivered nothing because the operating model beneath the org chart stayed the same.

The second mistake is abandoning middle management during the transition. Middle managers often feel threatened—they see new structures and metrics and think their relevance is disappearing. Make them your partners instead. They're your leverage point. If frontline managers understand and believe in the model, execution follows. If they're skeptical or threatened, they'll teach their teams to game the system rather than thrive in it. Effective [change management](https://www.prosci.com/change-management) requires engaging middle management early. I've seen organisations hire consultants to design brilliant operating models, then watch them fail because middle management didn't buy in.

The third mistake is designing for perfection. There's no perfect operating model. The more time you spend optimizing for every edge case, the more obsolete your design becomes. Design for 80 percent. Launch it. Learn from real use. Refine. A living 80-percent model beats a dead 99-percent one.

## The Operating Model as Your AI Strategy: The Agent Imperative

AI agents change the game entirely. Unlike traditional AI models that provide recommendations, agents are autonomous systems that perceive, decide, and act—often without real-time human oversight. They can operate 24/7, make thousands of decisions, access multiple systems, and execute actions. The capacity multiplier is extraordinary.

But here's what most organisations aren't ready for: agents expose massive gaps in traditional operating models.

Traditional roles assume humans make decisions. With agents, decisions happen autonomously. Your role definitions now require extension—you're no longer defining "who decides," you're defining "who sets the parameters agents operate within" and "who monitors agent behavior." These are fundamentally different capabilities.

Traditional accountability assumes someone owns an outcome. With agents, when an agent makes a pricing decision, approves credit, or flags a customer for retention, who is accountable if it's wrong? The person who set the parameters? The person who approved the agent deployment? The person who built the agent? If you don't answer this clearly before the agent goes live, you'll discover the answer in a crisis when something goes wrong.

Traditional data governance assumes you need access control. With agents, you need quality guarantees. An agent making thousands of decisions based on bad data amplifies that badness at scale. I've watched organisations deploy agents against datasets they didn't fully understand, with quality standards they hadn't validated, and with no one accountable for what the agent was actually seeing. The agent's decisions looked reasonable until we discovered the underlying data was corrupted or incomplete. The agent was working exactly as designed—just with bad inputs.

Traditional oversight assumes human review. With agents, you need monitoring systems and circuit breakers. What happens if an agent detects a pattern you didn't anticipate? How quickly can you shut it down? What alerts tell you something is going wrong? Can the agent correct course, or does it require human intervention? These aren't nice-to-have safeguards—they're foundational to safe agent deployment.

Traditional tool access is function-specific. Agents need broader tool access—they might need to read from multiple databases, write to multiple systems, trigger actions across the organisation. Your operating model's technology component now needs to specify not just "who can access what data," but "what systems can agents access, under what conditions, with what constraints?" This is new territory for most organisations.

This is why agents require operating model extension, not just iteration. You can't deploy agents at scale within a traditional operating model. You need new role definitions (parameter setters, agent monitors, quality validators), new accountability frameworks (what happens when an agent errs?), new data governance (quality guarantees, not just access control), and [new oversight mechanisms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08379) (real-time monitoring, correction, rollback capability).

Organisations that treat agents as "just another AI project" and deploy them within their existing operating models will discover problems at scale. Organisations that redesign their operating models explicitly for agents—clarifying who owns agent behavior, who monitors it, what data standards agents rely on, and what safeguards are in place—will scale them safely and capture the capacity multiplier they offer.

## The Operating Model as Your Competitive Moat

A well-defined operating model is no longer a luxury—it's essential. It's the difference between organisations that execute brilliantly and those consumed by complexity.

For years, ambiguity was survivable. Misaligned incentives, unclear ownership, broken handoffs—they created waste, but people worked around them. Heroic effort compensated for dysfunctional systems.

That changes with autonomous agents. When you deploy agents, you're no longer asking people to compensate for system gaps. You're asking autonomous systems to operate within your operating model. Agents don't make workarounds. They expose every gap, every ambiguity, every misalignment—at scale.

An agent given unclear data ownership will amplify data quality problems across thousands of decisions. An agent given misaligned metrics will optimise ruthlessly for the wrong outcomes. An agent deployed without clear accountability will make decisions nobody can defend. Operating models that survived through human flexibility will fail under autonomous decision-making.

The organisations that thrive will be the ones that redesigned their operating models explicitly for agents. They clarified roles, data ownership, decision rights, and oversight mechanisms. They built systems that don't require human workarounds.

Your operating model used to be about coordination. It's now about control and trust. Can you trust an autonomous system operating within your model? Can you explain its decisions? Can you correct course if it goes wrong?

The organisations that answer these questions well will capture the capacity multiplier agents offer. The ones that don't will spend years remediating decisions that autonomous systems made at scale—decisions they can no longer explain or undo.

Your operating model is your governance framework for autonomous systems. Get it right, and transformation follows. Get it wrong, and you'll be managing the consequences for years.
